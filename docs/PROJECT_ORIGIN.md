## 1. Executive Summary

This project began with a simple real-world observation during a visit
to Pizza House.

Pizza House is a modern, reputable, and high-quality restaurant with a
strong focus on pizza and related products such as toppings, add-ons,
drinks, desserts, and other restaurant offerings. The restaurant already
has the most important foundations of a successful food business: a
physical location, a kitchen, staff, products, customers, and an
established local presence.

However, one practical problem became noticeable during the visit:
customers could experience a waiting period of approximately 10, 15, 20
minutes, or potentially longer before receiving their orders, depending
on workload and circumstances.

The important insight was not that the restaurant was poor. The opposite
was true: the restaurant was good enough that improving the ordering
experience could make the overall experience even better.

This created the central question:

> **Why should a customer have to arrive first, wait to order, and then
> wait for preparation when the customer could browse the menu,
> customize an order, submit it in advance, select a preferred arrival
> or pickup time, and potentially pay before arriving?**

That question became the foundation of the project.

The original idea was therefore not simply to create a restaurant
website. It evolved into a **digital ordering and restaurant operations
platform**.

The first implementation is intended to serve Pizza House as a
real-world pilot. The long-term strategic goal is to learn from that
implementation and evolve the solution into a reusable product called
**Novixa Restaurant** for other small and medium-sized restaurants.

# 2. How the Idea Started

## 2.1 The Real-World Starting Point

The idea did not begin as a theoretical SaaS concept.

It began with a real customer experience.

During a visit to Pizza House, the restaurant appeared to be a strong
candidate for digital improvement. It was modern, reputable, and
specialized in pizza and related food products.

The menu naturally lends itself to digital ordering:

-   Different pizza types.
-   Pizza sizes and variants.
-   Additional toppings and add-ons.
-   Drinks.
-   Desserts.
-   Combos and possible promotional offers.
-   Other food items.

But the ordering process could involve waiting.

Depending on circumstances, a customer might wait:

-   10 minutes.
-   15 minutes.
-   20 minutes.
-   Or potentially longer during busy periods.

This observation led to a broader question:

> **Can technology move part of the ordering process from the restaurant
> counter to the customer's phone before the customer arrives?**

That question is the origin of this project.

# 3. The Core Problem

The project is not fundamentally about creating a beautiful restaurant
website.

It is about reducing friction between:

**Customer intention → Order → Restaurant preparation → Customer arrival
→ Food handoff**

A traditional workflow may look like:

1.  Customer decides to visit the restaurant.
2.  Customer travels to the restaurant.
3.  Customer arrives and waits.
4.  Customer views the menu.
5.  Customer decides what to order.
6.  Customer communicates the order to staff.
7.  Staff records the order.
8.  Payment is handled.
9.  Kitchen receives the order.
10. Kitchen prepares the food.
11. Customer waits.
12. Customer receives the order.

The proposed digital workflow is:

1.  Customer opens the Pizza House ordering website.
2.  Customer browses the menu before arriving.
3.  Customer selects products.
4.  Customer customizes products.
5.  Customer adds items and add-ons.
6.  Customer selects pickup or, in a future phase, delivery.
7.  Customer chooses ASAP or a scheduled pickup/arrival time.
8.  Customer selects a payment method.
9.  Customer submits the order.
10. Restaurant receives the order digitally.
11. The system determines when preparation should begin.
12. Kitchen receives the order at the appropriate time.
13. Staff prepares the order.
14. Customer arrives.
15. Order is ready or nearly ready.
16. Customer receives the order with less unnecessary waiting.

The goal is therefore not simply:

> **Order online.**

The deeper goal is:

> **Prepare the right order at the right time so the customer can have a
> smoother restaurant experience.**

# 4. The First Major Product Insight: Scheduled Ordering

One of the most important ideas that emerged from the original
observation is **scheduled ordering**.

Imagine a customer opens the website at **4:00 PM**.

The customer wants to visit the restaurant at **8:00 PM**.

Instead of waiting until 8:00 PM to order, the customer can:

-   Browse the menu at 4:00 PM.
-   Select a pizza.
-   Select size and toppings.
-   Add drinks.
-   Add dessert.
-   Confirm the order.
-   Choose **8:00 PM** as the desired pickup/arrival time.

Suppose the estimated preparation time is 15--20 minutes.

The restaurant should not necessarily prepare the order immediately at
4:00 PM.

Instead, the system calculates a preparation window.

Example:

**Desired pickup:** 8:00 PM\
**Estimated preparation:** 15 minutes\
**Planned preparation start:** approximately 7:45 PM

The relevant restaurant staff can receive a reminder at the appropriate
time.

This creates a simple but powerful principle:

> **The customer orders early, but the restaurant prepares at the
> correct time.**

This means the system must distinguish between:

-   Order creation time.
-   Requested pickup time.
-   Planned preparation time.
-   Actual preparation time.
-   Ready time.
-   Completion time.

That distinction is fundamental to the product.

# 5. Why Scheduled Ordering Matters

Scheduled ordering can create value for several groups.

## Customer Benefits

Customers can:

-   Decide what they want before arriving.
-   Avoid spending time reading the menu at the restaurant.
-   Avoid repeating complicated orders verbally.
-   Customize their food in advance.
-   Know the expected pickup time.
-   Potentially pay before arrival.
-   Reduce uncertainty.
-   Spend more of their visit enjoying the restaurant rather than
    waiting.

## Restaurant Benefits

The restaurant can:

-   Receive orders earlier.
-   Plan preparation more effectively.
-   Reduce counter congestion.
-   Improve kitchen coordination.
-   Better understand upcoming demand.
-   Prepare ingredients and workload more intelligently.
-   Reduce ordering mistakes caused by verbal communication.
-   Organize peak-hour demand.
-   Create a more professional digital workflow.

## Business Benefits

The restaurant also gains a direct digital relationship with customers.

Instead of relying entirely on:

-   Walk-in traffic.
-   Phone calls.
-   Social media messages.
-   Third-party delivery platforms.

the restaurant can gradually build its own direct ordering channel.

# 12. Order Timing Model

Timing is one of the most important technical and business concepts in
the project.

An order should have separate timestamps such as:

-   Created at.
-   Requested pickup time.
-   Estimated preparation duration.
-   Planned preparation start.
-   Actual preparation start.
-   Ready time.
-   Completion time.

Example:

**Order created:** 4:00 PM\
**Requested pickup:** 8:00 PM\
**Preparation duration:** 15 minutes\
**Planned preparation:** 7:45 PM\
**Actual preparation:** 7:44 PM\
**Ready:** 7:59 PM\
**Customer arrives:** 8:00 PM\
**Completed:** 8:03 PM

The exact timing must be configurable.

The AI implementation must not hard-code assumptions such as 15 minutes.
Preparation times should be stored as configurable business data.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 13. Order Capacity

Scheduled ordering introduces another important concept: **capacity**.

A restaurant cannot necessarily accept unlimited orders for the same
time window.

Example configuration:

-   10 orders per 15-minute window.

If the 7:00 PM slot reaches capacity, the system should:

-   Mark that slot as full.
-   Prevent additional orders for that slot.
-   Suggest the next available slot.

Capacity can eventually consider:

-   Number of orders.
-   Estimated preparation workload.
-   Product complexity.
-   Kitchen capacity.
-   Staff availability.

The first version can use simple configurable capacity rules. Advanced
demand-aware capacity can come later.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 14. Pause Online Ordering

Restaurants sometimes become temporarily overloaded.

Examples:

-   Kitchen is full.
-   Staff shortage.
-   Unexpected rush.
-   Equipment problem.
-   Ingredient shortage.
-   Temporary operational issue.

The restaurant manager should be able to:

**Pause online ordering**

When paused, the system should:

-   Stop new online orders.
-   Display a clear customer message.
-   Preserve access to existing orders.
-   Allow staff to resume ordering when ready.

This is an operational feature, not merely a UI feature.

# 18. Transfer Receipt Verification

For markets where bank or exchange transfers are common, customers may
upload payment receipts.

The first implementation should use:

**Manual verification by staff.**

Staff can:

1.  Open the payment proof.
2.  Check the amount.
3.  Check transaction/reference information.
4.  Confirm the transfer.
5.  Approve or reject the payment.

A later version can introduce AI-assisted OCR.

AI could extract:

-   Amount.
-   Date.
-   Reference number.
-   Sender name.
-   Recipient information.

However:

> **AI should initially assist human verification, not blindly authorize
> financial transactions.**

# 19. Order State Machine

A clear order lifecycle is essential.

A possible core lifecycle is:

**PENDING → PAYMENT_PENDING → CONFIRMED → QUEUED → PREPARING → READY →
COMPLETED**

Additional states:

-   CANCELLED
-   REJECTED
-   REFUNDED

The implementation should define valid transitions and authorization for
each transition.

Examples:

-   Customer can request cancellation under defined conditions.
-   Cashier can verify payment.
-   Kitchen staff can start preparation.
-   Kitchen staff can mark an order ready.
-   Staff or manager can complete an order.
-   Manager can override certain states according to permissions.

# 33. Security Principles

The platform will handle customer, order, and potentially
payment-related information.

Security must therefore be designed from the beginning.

Important requirements include:

-   Server-side authorization.
-   Role-based access control.
-   Secure sessions/cookies.
-   Input validation.
-   Safe output handling.
-   Rate limiting.
-   CSRF protection where applicable.
-   Secure file-upload validation.
-   Private payment-receipt storage.
-   Access-controlled receipt viewing.
-   IDOR prevention.
-   Audit logging.
-   Secure password handling where passwords are used.
-   No sensitive secrets in client-side code.
-   Server-side payment amount validation.
-   Order ownership checks.
-   Protection against duplicate submissions.
-   Safe error handling.
-   Secure database access.
-   Backup strategy.

Security must never depend on hiding UI buttons.

# 49. Risks and Mitigation

## Risk 1 --- Building Too Much

**Mitigation:** Start with the smallest valuable workflow.

## Risk 2 --- Customers Do Not Adopt It

**Mitigation:** Make ordering extremely simple and promote it through QR
codes, social media, signage, and direct links.

## Risk 3 --- Staff Do Not Use the Dashboard

**Mitigation:** Design staff workflows around real operations and
minimize clicks.

## Risk 4 --- Scheduled Orders Create Kitchen Confusion

**Mitigation:** Use clear queue states and preparation-start logic.

## Risk 5 --- Payment Verification Becomes a Bottleneck

**Mitigation:** Keep manual verification simple and later automate
assistance where useful.

## Risk 6 --- Existing POS Conflicts

**Mitigation:** Run alongside the existing system initially and
integrate only after discovery.

## Risk 7 --- Delivery Adds Excessive Complexity

**Mitigation:** Keep pickup as the initial focus.

## Risk 8 --- The Product Becomes Too Generic

**Mitigation:** Solve Pizza House's real problem first, then generalize
based on evidence.

# 59. Final Product Narrative

The complete story of the project can be summarized as follows:

A visit to a modern and reputable pizza restaurant revealed a simple but
meaningful operational problem: customers could spend 10, 15, 20 minutes
or more waiting for their orders.

The observation created a question:

> **Why should customers wait at the restaurant to perform an ordering
> process that could happen before they arrive?**

That question led to the idea of a digital ordering system.

The customer could open a website, browse the menu, choose pizza and
other products, customize the order, select payment, and choose when
they intend to arrive.

If the customer orders at 4:00 PM and chooses 8:00 PM pickup, the
restaurant does not necessarily prepare the order immediately. Instead,
the system calculates when preparation should begin and alerts the
relevant staff at the appropriate time.

This transforms the restaurant from a purely reactive ordering workflow
into a more predictable, scheduled workflow.

From there, the idea expanded naturally.

The website becomes a digital presence for Pizza House.

The menu becomes interactive.

Products become configurable.

Orders become trackable.

Payments become manageable.

The kitchen receives structured orders.

Customers receive status updates.

The restaurant can pause online ordering during overload.

Future versions can add delivery, reviews, loyalty, promotions,
WhatsApp, analytics, and AI-assisted management.

Eventually, the lessons learned from Pizza House can become a reusable
product:

# Novixa Restaurant

The broader vision is to provide small and medium-sized restaurants with
a modern digital ordering layer that connects customers, restaurant
staff, kitchen operations, payments, pickup, and eventually delivery.

The project therefore has two identities:

### Immediate Identity

**Pizza House Digital Ordering & Operations System**

A real-world solution for a specific restaurant.

### Long-Term Identity

**Novixa Restaurant**

A reusable restaurant technology product built from real-world
operational knowledge.
